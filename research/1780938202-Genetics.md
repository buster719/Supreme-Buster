## Research-chain note: mandatory genetic counseling before PRS return and lower-SES uptake

### 1. Follow-up questions

- Are there alternative support models (e.g. digital decision aids, group counseling, community health worker involvement) that preserve informed consent without acting as gatekeeping bottlenecks?

- How strongly does PRS-based risk information actually change health behavior in different socioeconomic strata, making the counseling “mandate” either critical or a net-negative?

### 3. Working answer

It seems plausible that making genetic counseling a mandatory precondition for receiving PRS results could inadvertently create demand-side barriers that disproportionately reduce uptake in lower-SES groups. This is not a strong conclusion yet, because only sparse direct evidence on mandatory PRS-specific counseling exists. The reasoning arises from several intersecting lines:

First, genetic counseling faces well-documented access barriers even when optional: travel distance, financial cost (in systems where it isn’t fully covered), time away from hourly work, and language discordance. Lower-SES individuals are already less likely to complete counseling when it is offered before testing. Making counseling mandatory doesn’t eliminate these barriers; it turns them into a hard gate that can exclude those unable to comply.

Second, in other domains (e.g., mandatory in-person visits before receiving certain medications or benefits), tying a desired outcome to a high-friction requirement can depress uptake among groups with fewer resources, turning a protective measure into a de facto exclusion mechanism.
